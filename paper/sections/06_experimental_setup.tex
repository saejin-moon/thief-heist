\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\subsection{Curriculum Design \& Progressive Scaling}
The HEIST benchmark evaluates policy transfer, scalability, and specialization across a five-stage progressive curriculum (Table~\ref{tab:stage_specs}). Environmental complexity scales from compact $11 \times 11$ single-room layouts with zero security elements (Stage 0) to vast $50 \times 50$ facility floorplans featuring 4 patrolling guards, 3 rotating security cameras, 4 locked security doors, a single master security terminal, and a single loot vault, with a maximum horizon of $T_{\max} = 5,\!000$ steps (Stage 4). Every stage retains exactly one master security terminal and one loot vault; only the guard, camera, and door counts, the episode horizon, and the alarm tolerance scale with the curriculum. Network checkpoints from preceding curriculum stages serve as initializations for subsequent stages, testing whether architectures can absorb escalating operational demands without catastrophic forgetting. This transfer is enabled by a fixed-size centralized state representation shared across all stages: the grid channel is embedded on the maximum $50 \times 50$ canvas (cells outside the active stage's floorplan are filled with the wall token) and adversarial elements are padded to fixed capacity (12 guard slots, with unused slots marked $-1$), so the centralized critic input dimensionality is identical at every stage and checkpoints transfer without any architectural modification. Local observations are likewise stage-independent: each agent receives a fixed $7 \times 7$ egocentric window. Training budgets scale across stages ($2 \times 10^5$ to $1.6 \times 10^6$ environment steps per stage; $3.4 \times 10^6$ steps in total per seed), with full update schedules listed in Table~\ref{tab:hyperparameters}.

\subsection{Evaluated Algorithms: Method Lineage and External Baselines}
Our empirical benchmark evaluates nine distinct algorithms organized into our iterative development lineage and external MARL baselines:

\paragraph{Iterative Development Lineage (Ours):}
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,nosep]
    \item \textbf{MARC (Iteration 1: Credit Assignment):} Monolithic CTDE PPO augmented with affordance-aware potential-based reward shaping for heterogeneous subtasks.
    \item \textbf{COOP (Iteration 2: Static Modular Capacity):} Introduces multi-expert capacity with $K=2$ fixed experts and unconstrained scalar value bidding, devoid of evolutionary or incubation mechanisms.
    \item \textbf{E-COOP (Iteration 3: Evolutionary Macro-Ablation):} Evolutionary PPO employing periodic Fisher-weighted parameter recombination with critic cloning, consolidating parameters into a single champion policy ($K=1$).
    \item \textbf{THIEF (Ours, Final Framework):} Dynamic Mixture-of-Experts with win-rate plateau triggering, failure deficit mutation, all-pool Fisher recombination, isolated sandbox incubation, and hysteresis-bounded load-balanced routing.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{External MARL Baselines:}
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,nosep]
    \item \textbf{ROMA}~\cite{wang2020roma}: Role-Oriented Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning with Emergent Roles, learning dynamic continuous role latent vectors via an observation-conditioned role encoder regularized by identifiability, temporal compactness, and cross-agent dissimilarity losses.
    \item \textbf{RODE}~\cite{wang2021rode}: Role-Oriented Action Decomposition for Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning, which learns action representations from forward transition dynamics, clusters discrete actions into semantic role subsets, and employs a macro-step role selector to constrain policy execution.
    \item \textbf{MAPPO}~\cite{yu2022surprising}: Standard Centralized Training with Decentralized Execution PPO utilizing a shared centralized value function with Generalized Advantage Estimation (GAE).
    \item \textbf{H-MAPPO}: Our adaptation of FeUdal Networks~\cite{vezhnevets2017feudal} to the MAPPO~\cite{yu2022surprising} framework: a two-level hierarchical architecture where a high-level Manager policy generates 2D directional sub-goals every $\Delta t = 10$ macro-steps for low-level execution policies.
    \item \textbf{COMA}~\cite{foerster2018counterfactual}: Counterfactual Multi-Agent Policy Gradients using a centralized state-action critic $Q(s, \mathbf{u})$ to marginalize individual agent actions and compute agent-specific counterfactual baselines.
\end{enumerate}

All nine algorithms are evaluated on the full HEIST curriculum under the identical training and evaluation protocol and are reported together in Table~\ref{tab:main_results}; Section~\ref{sec:results} additionally reports ROMA and RODE on the Level-Based Foraging testbed (Table~\ref{tab:lbf_benchmark}) to probe generalization beyond our custom environment.

\subsection{High-Throughput Rust Engine \& Evaluation Protocol}
All neural policies utilize identical architectural backbones: 2-layer MLPs with 64 hidden units and Tanh activations (detailed in Table~\ref{tab:hyperparameters}). The HEIST simulation engine is implemented in native multi-threaded Rust using Rayon for parallel pathfinding and line-of-sight raytracing, bridged to PyTorch CUDA tensors via PyO3 with zero-copy contiguous buffer transfers. On our evaluation workstation (Intel Core i9-10980XE 18-core/36-thread processor, NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3080 Ti GPU with 12 GB VRAM), the raw simulation engine sustains approximately $11,\!500$--$12,\!500$ environment steps per second across the five curriculum stages.

To guarantee research-grade statistical rigor, all HEIST algorithms are trained across \textbf{10 independent random seeds} (seeds 0--9). Evaluation is performed over \textbf{1,000 independent held-out episodes per checkpoint} across all 450 checkpoints (9 algorithms $\times$ 5 stages $\times$ 10 seeds), yielding \textbf{450,000 total evaluation episodes}. In evaluation, actions are stochastically sampled from policy categorical distributions (`Categorical.sample()`), and expert selection follows greedy argmax value bidding constrained by the hysteresis barrier.

\subsection{Canonical Heterogeneous Benchmark: Level-Based Foraging (LBF)}
\label{sec:setup_lbf}
To evaluate whether THIEF's architectural advantages generalize beyond the HEIST domain to standard peer-reviewed benchmarks in the cooperative MARL literature, we evaluate all methods on the canonical Level-Based Foraging (LBF) testbed~\cite{papoudakis2021benchmarking}. LBF models mixed-coordination cooperative foraging where agents possess asymmetric capability levels and must coordinate spatial navigation and simultaneous action execution (\texttt{LOAD}) to collect food items. We benchmark two standardized multi-agent configurations:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,nosep]
    \item \textbf{Foraging-8$\times$8 (2p, 3f, Asymmetric Levels):} Two asymmetric players (levels 1--2) encounter three multi-level food items. Foods require combined player levels to collect, mixing solo and cooperative collection strategies.
    \item \textbf{Foraging-8$\times$8 (3p, 3f, Asymmetric):} Three asymmetric players (levels $[1, 2, 2]$) encounter three multi-level foods (levels up to 5). The highest-level food requires dynamic whole-team rendezvous ($1 + 2 + 2 = 5$), directly testing adaptive role assignment. All methods are trained across 5 independent random seeds with 100 evaluation episodes per seed.
\end{enumerate}
